Historical Note

Castellammare’s Eagle and Swabian / Hohenstaufen Sicily

This note provides historical context for the Sicilian eagle tradition, especially in western Sicily, including “Ghibelline” heraldry, Calatafimi-Segesta, and the Hohenstaufen period.

← Back to the Three Eagle Styles

The most likely historical setting

The golden eagle in the civic arms of Castellammare del Golfo is best understood within the political and heraldic world of Swabian/Hohenstaufen Sicily, especially the age of Frederick II in the thirteenth century.

Castellammare’s official Italian blazon describes a red tower rising from the sea, merlato alla ghibellina—crenellated in the Ghibelline manner—and charged with a golden eagle gripping the top of the round-arched doorway. The combination of eagle + fortified tower + Ghibelline battlements is therefore part of the formal civic heraldry, not merely a modern stylistic interpretation.

Calatafimi-Segesta: the strongest local comparison

Nearby Calatafimi-Segesta preserves a remarkably similar visual vocabulary. The town developed around an older fortified site associated with the Arabic name Qalʿat Fīmī. Local historical tradition describes the civic emblem as recalling the castle, above whose towers “un’aquila sveva” — a Swabian eagle — spreads its wings.

This helps separate historical layers. The fortress and place-name can preserve an Islamic-period past while the eagle belongs to a later Swabian-imperial political vocabulary. The Arab period helps explain the fortified landscape; it does not provide the strongest explanation for the displayed heraldic eagle.

Frederick II and the imperial eagle

The Hohenstaufen inherited the Norman Kingdom of Sicily through Henry VI’s marriage to Constance of Sicily. Their son Frederick II became King of Sicily and later Holy Roman Emperor. Sicily and southern Italy remained central to his government.

Frederick’s use of the eagle is directly documented. His gold Augustalis, introduced in 1231 and minted in southern Italy including Messina, bears the emperor in Roman style on one side and an eagle on the reverse. The Metropolitan Museum of Art identifies the bird explicitly as “the imperial symbol.” An American Numismatic Society example likewise records an eagle with wings spread on the reverse.

Working conclusion: Castellammare’s eagle is best described as strongly associated with the Swabian/Hohenstaufen imperial heraldic tradition of medieval Sicily. The evidence does not yet prove that Frederick II personally granted Castellammare its eagle or that the modern municipal drawing is an unchanged thirteenth-century design.

What “Ghibelline” means

Ghibelline was the medieval Italian label for political factions that generally aligned themselves with the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor, conventionally contrasted with the Guelphs, who generally favored papal power. In practice, Italian cities also used these labels for local rivalries, so they should not be treated as a perfect ideological binary.

The name is traditionally connected with Waiblingen in present-day Baden-Württemberg, Germany. In later heraldic usage, Ghibelline battlements usually means the characteristic swallow-tailed or V-notched crenellations. Castellammare’s official blazon explicitly uses the phrase merlato alla ghibellina.

From Swabia to Sicily: places that survive today

Hohenstaufen / Göppingen, Germany. The mountain Hohenstaufen gave its name to the ancestral castle, then to the family and dynasty. The castle survives as ruins, and Göppingen maintains a permanent Staufer exhibition.

Waiblingen, Germany. Important to the traditional derivation of the Italian term “Ghibelline.” The city’s official history connects the medieval names with the later Italian Ghibelline/Guelph terminology.

Lorch Monastery, Germany. A principal Staufer memorial and dynastic burial site.

Castel del Monte, Puglia, Italy. Frederick II’s celebrated thirteenth-century monument. UNESCO emphasizes its synthesis of northern European, classical, and Islamic cultural elements.

Messina, Sicily. One of the mints associated with Frederick II’s Augustalis. The coin’s eagle is a direct Hohenstaufen-era imperial image tied to Sicily.

Calatafimi-Segesta, Sicily. The strongest nearby civic parallel: a castle-and-eagle emblem locally described as bearing a “Swabian eagle.”

Castellammare del Golfo, Sicily. The municipal arms preserve the distinctive combination of sea-fortress, golden eagle, and explicitly Ghibelline crenellation.
